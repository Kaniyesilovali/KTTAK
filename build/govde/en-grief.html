After a loss: on grief

Grief has no stages, no order and no timetable. This guide explains why grief after a disaster is heavier in particular ways, how to tell a child about a death, and how to stand beside someone who is grieving.

Frame

There is no such thing as five stages

Contrary to the popular belief, grief is not a sequence of stages worked through in order. That idea grew out of an older observation of how people with terminal illness responded to their own dying, and was later misapplied to the bereaved. The reality is messier: a good day follows a bad one, and anger and acceptance arrive within the same hour.

Knowing this matters in practice, because "I am grieving wrongly" is a second weight on top of the first. Grief has no measure. Not crying does not mean you did not love them; still crying months later does not mean you are stuck.

The frame more often used in the field today describes grief as an oscillation between turning towards the loss and turning back to life. One day a person deals with the loss itself; the next they pay a bill, go to work, laugh. Both are grief, and laughing is not a betrayal.

Disaster grief

Five things that make grief after a disaster heavier

A loss after an illness, where goodbyes were possible, is not the same as a loss in a disaster. Knowing the difference stops you from holding yourself to an unfair standard.

Sudden and unprepared

No goodbye, no last sentence. The mind was given none of the time it needs to accept the fact, so for weeks it feels "as though they will walk in".

Multiple losses

More than one person at once, and also the home, the street, the job, the neighbours. The mind cannot queue losses; they all land together.

Ambiguous loss

Someone unreachable, or identified only much later. There is neither room to grieve nor grounds to hope. This is the most corrosive form of grief and specifically calls for support.

Rituals cut short

A rushed funeral, a condolence gathering that could not happen. Ritual is not decoration; it is the social machinery that makes a loss real and shares its weight.

Survivor guilt

"Why did I get out and they didn't." This thought cannot be argued away with logic, it is extremely common, and it begins to lose weight when it is said out loud.

Grief's turn never comes

Shelter, children, paperwork, work. Grief is postponed — and usually returns months later, precisely when things look "sorted" from the outside. That delay is normal.

Children

Telling a child about a death

Someone familiar, in plain words

An adult the child trusts should tell them, somewhere quiet, holding them or sitting beside them. Do not build up to it; give the fact in a single sentence, then stop. "I have something very sad to tell you. Grandad died in the earthquake." The rest moves at the child's pace.

Use the word "died"

"We lost him", "he's asleep", "he left us" confuse a young child: things that are lost can be found, people who sleep wake up. Explain concretely what death is — the body has stopped working, it feels no pain, and it does not come back.

Say plainly that it is not their fault

Children between three and six in particular may believe that a moment of anger caused the death. Do not guess at what goes unsaid; say it directly: "This is not something anybody did. None of it is your fault."

Offer the funeral as a choice

Neither push nor forbid. Describe in advance what will happen: who will be there, how long it will take, that people may cry. Appoint one adult who will stay with them throughout and take them outside if needed. For a child who wants to attend, ritual helps as much as it helps an adult.

Grief arrives through play

A child may go back to playing ten minutes after hearing the news. That is not indifference; children grieve in small pieces with play in between. In adolescents, grief more often shows up as anger or withdrawal.

Standing beside them

What to say to someone grieving, and what not to

The sentences on the left try to shrink the pain or find a meaning in it; what they usually leave behind is isolation. Those on the right claim less, and that is exactly why they work.

  • Avoid"You have to be strong. You need to hold it together for the children."

    Instead"I can't imagine how hard this is. I'm here."

  • Avoid"At least the others survived."

    Instead"You miss him. I'll listen if you want to talk, and I'll sit here if you don't."

  • Avoid"Everything happens for a reason."

    InsteadSilence. A grieving person searches for meaning in their own time; meaning brought in from outside usually produces anger.

  • Avoid"Call me if you need anything."

    Instead"I'm bringing food on Thursday evening and leaving it at the door." — Someone grieving cannot describe what they need; offer something specific.

  • AvoidAfter three months, never mentioning them, never saying the name.

    InsteadSay the name: "I was thinking about Ayşe a lot today." The loneliest part, bereaved people say most often, is that everyone avoids the name.

The first week

Food, childcare, paperwork, answering the phone. Do tasks rather than talk.

The first six months

Loneliness begins once the crowd disperses. The real support is needed here: regular, quiet, unpushy contact.

Anniversaries

Birthdays, religious holidays, the anniversary of the disaster. Send a message that day; "I haven't forgotten" is enough on its own.

When to seek help

Grief is natural; not every grief can be carried alone

Not everyone who grieves needs therapy. But if the following are still intense after roughly a year, or if they bring life to a halt sooner than that, a specialist should be involved.

  • No movement at all towards accepting the loss; continuing to deny the death
  • Being unable to touch any of their belongings — or unable to let go of anything
  • Daily life stopped for months: no return to work, school or the running of the home
  • Systematic avoidance of everything that recalls the person
  • Intense guilt or anger that does not subside
  • Rising alcohol or substance use
  • Wanting to die, thoughts of "joining them" — do not wait on this one

Support matters especially for families living with ambiguous loss — those whose relative was never found. In that situation grief cannot complete itself unaided.
