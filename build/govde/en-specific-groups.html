Specific groups

The same crisis does not affect everyone in the same way. Different needs and different approaches for children, older people, people with disabilities, migrants and responders.

On this page

  • What it is

  • What you can do
  • When to seek support

01

What it is

International standards stress that in crises some groups face greater risk and need particular effort to reach. The reason is not the groups themselves but the conditions that leave them out: inaccessible information, unsuitable shelter, interrupted care.

Inclusive psychosocial support treats these groups not as an add-on but as people considered from the very start of planning.

Children and young people

They often show their reactions through behaviour rather than words: regression, clinging, anger, falling school performance. Routine, a safe adult and play are the strongest protective factors.

Older people

Disruption to medication and care, limited mobility and loneliness raise the risk. They need to be considered in advance in information and evacuation plans.

People with disabilities

Warning, evacuation and shelter processes are often not designed with them in mind. Accessible information and taking part in decisions are basic rights.

Pregnant women and new parents

Access to health care and caring for a baby are extra sources of worry in a crisis. Mental health in the postnatal period needs particular attention.

Migrants and people facing language barriers

Not understanding warnings, being unable to reach services and legal uncertainty increase vulnerability. Information should be given in more than one language.

Responders

Firefighters, health workers, teachers and volunteers carry a risk of secondary trauma while supporting others.

02

Expected reactions

  • Trouble falling asleep, waking often, nightmares
  • Startling at sudden noises, feeling constantly on guard
  • Going over the event again and again — or not wanting to think about it at all
  • Restlessness, anger, crying spells, emotional numbness
  • Difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness
  • Headaches, stomach problems, muscle tension, fatigue
  • Guilt, shame or “why did I survive” thoughts

How reactions show varies with age, culture and the person’s way of communicating; the distress of a child or of an adult with a communication disability may appear in behaviour rather than words.

03

What you can do

  • Ask the person what they need; do not assume.
  • Give information in plain language, in more than one format (written, spoken, visual) and, where needed, in more than one language.
  • Include carers and families in the support plan.
  • Apply child-safeguarding principles in all work involving children.
  • Plan rest, rotation and peer support for response teams.

04

When to seek support

  • Reactions last longer than four weeks or keep getting worse
  • Daily tasks — work, school, caring for children — have become impossible
  • Use of alcohol, medication or other substances has increased
  • The person is completely withdrawing and not talking to anyone
  • There are thoughts of harming oneself or others — in this case, without waiting

Do not wait: if danger is ongoing, or you or someone else is thinking about self-harm, call 112 now; for violence call 155. TAK-DER is not a crisis line; see where to turn.
