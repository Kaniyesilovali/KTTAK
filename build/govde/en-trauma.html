Trauma

A sudden loss, an accident, an assault or a life-threatening event: experiences that shake a person’s sense of safety, and what may follow.

On this page

  • Before / During / After
  • What to do

01

What it is

A traumatic event is one in which a person experiences, witnesses or learns that their own life or someone else’s is in danger. The same event does not leave the same mark on everyone; its severity, the person’s history and the support they receive shape the outcome.

Reactions after trauma are not a sign of weakness; they are the mind and body trying to protect themselves from danger. Most people recover with time and support.

Information on violence and social conflict is on a separate page: Violence and social conflict.

02

Expected reactions

  • Trouble falling asleep, waking often, nightmares
  • Startling at sudden noises, feeling constantly on guard
  • Going over the event again and again — or not wanting to think about it at all
  • Restlessness, anger, crying spells, emotional numbness
  • Difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness
  • Headaches, stomach problems, muscle tension, fatigue
  • Guilt, shame or “why did I survive” thoughts
  • Avoiding places, people or conversations that bring back the event

These are normal reactions to an abnormal event. For most people they ease noticeably within a few weeks, with safety, routine and social support.

03

Before, during and after

Before

  • Strong social ties and a reliable support network are the most important protective factors.
  • Knowing in advance about trauma reactions makes what follows easier to understand.
  • Having people trained in psychological first aid in institutions and schools.

During

  • Safety first: move away from danger and call the emergency lines.
  • Be a calm, reassuring presence next to the person.
  • Give verified information; do not promise what cannot be promised.

After

  • Know that reactions in the first weeks are normal, and return to daily routines.
  • Stay connected; avoid isolation.
  • Seek specialist support if reactions persist or worsen.

04

What to do

  • Safety first: make sure the person is safe and their basic needs are met.
  • Listen, but do not push them to talk or press for details.
  • Try to bring back daily routines — sleep, meals, short walks — in small steps.
  • Stay connected with loved ones; try not to be alone.
  • Avoid alcohol and stimulants; they may bring short relief but slow recovery.
  • Notice your own feelings and limits too; carrying someone else’s burden is tiring.

05

When to seek support and where to turn

  • Reactions last longer than four weeks or get worse
  • Daily tasks such as going to work or school or caring for children become impossible
  • Use of alcohol, medication or other substances increases
  • The person isolates themselves completely and talks to no one
  • There are thoughts of harming oneself or others — in this case, without waiting

If life is in danger

Do not wait: call 112, or 155 for violence. All emergency numbers

For specialist support

Your family doctor or the mental health units of state hospitals can refer you to the right service.
