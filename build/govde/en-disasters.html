Disasters

Earthquakes, fires, floods, extreme heat or epidemics: shocks too severe for a community to cope with on its own, and the psychological marks they leave.

01

What it is

UNDRR defines a disaster as a serious disruption of the functioning of a community, arising when a hazard interacts with people’s exposure, vulnerability and capacity. A natural event alone does not make a disaster; who it hits, and how prepared they are, does.

Environmental crises — droughts, heatwaves, water scarcity — often unfold slowly, yet through prolonged uncertainty, lost livelihoods and displacement they create a similar psychosocial burden.

Most people affected by a disaster recover over time. The aim of psychosocial support is to protect and strengthen this natural recovery and to notice in time those who need more help.

02

Expected reactions

  • Trouble falling asleep, waking often, nightmares
  • Startling at sudden noises, feeling constantly on guard
  • Going over the event again and again — or not wanting to think about it at all
  • Restlessness, anger, crying spells, emotional numbness
  • Difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness
  • Headaches, stomach problems, muscle tension, fatigue
  • Guilt, shame or “why did I survive” thoughts

These are normal reactions to an abnormal event. For most people they ease noticeably within a few weeks, with safety, routine and social support.

03

Before, during and after

Before

  • Prepare a family disaster plan and an emergency kit.
  • Reduce non-structural risks at home.
  • Talk to children in advance, in age-appropriate language.

During

  • Safety first; follow the guidance of official agencies.
  • Get in touch with loved ones; take information from official sources.
  • Support those around you using psychological first aid principles.

After

  • Bring back sleep, meals and daily routines in small steps.
  • Limit news exposure; keep social ties.
  • Seek specialist support if reactions persist or worsen.

04

What to do

  • Safety first: until the danger has passed, follow official guidance (Civil Defence, 112).
  • Reconnect with your loved ones; tell them where you are and find out how they are.
  • Try to bring back sleep, meals and daily routine, even in small steps.
  • Limit news and social media; get information from official sources.
  • Share your feelings with someone you trust — but do not force yourself or others to talk.
  • Helping others is good for you — while respecting your own limits.
  • Avoid alcohol and stimulants; they bring short-term relief but slow recovery.

05

When to seek support and where to turn

  • Reactions last longer than four weeks or keep getting worse
  • Daily tasks — work, school, caring for children — have become impossible
  • Use of alcohol, medication or other substances has increased
  • The person is completely withdrawing and not talking to anyone
  • There are thoughts of harming oneself or others — in this case, without waiting

If life is in danger

Do not wait: call 112, or 155 for violence. All emergency numbers

For specialist support

Your family doctor or the mental health units of state hospitals can refer you to the right service.
