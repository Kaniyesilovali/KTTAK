Five areas of work, one chain

Our programmes feed each other: volunteers trained in one join field coordination in another, what we learn in the field returns to the resource centre, and the gaps in those resources set our advocacy agenda.

Preparedness Field coordination Psychosocial support Capacity building Advocacy

Area 01

Preparedness

Cyprus's risk profile cannot be reduced to a single hazard: seismic risk along the Cyprus arc, forest fires that return every summer, lengthening droughts and flash flooding are all part of the same preparedness work. We run hands-on training in schools, workplaces and neighbourhood units — designed as a process that ends in a drill, not a presentation that ends in applause.

Where
Schools, workplaces, neighbourhood units
Partners
Ministry of National Education, municipalities, neighbourhood offices
Cost
Free for state schools and neighbourhood units

Programme components

  • Basic disaster literacy: reading risk maps, how buildings behave, drop-cover-hold practice, fire evacuation.
  • Family disaster plan: meeting point, out-of-town contact, emergency bag contents.
  • Evacuation drills: run in real time, with accessibility for disabled occupants built in.
  • Neighbourhood disaster volunteer: 24 hours of basic training plus annual refreshers.
  • Early years module: play-based, non-frightening material for ages 4–6.

Area 02

Field coordination

In the first days after a disaster the biggest problem is rarely a lack of resources — it is resources going to the wrong place. Our teams run needs assessment, manage volunteer flow and prevent duplicated aid.

Response time
Assessment team within 24 hours
Coverage
Teams based in Nicosia, Famagusta and Kyrenia

What we do in the field, and what we don't

We do

  • Household-level needs assessment and registration
  • Setting up and running child-friendly spaces
  • Volunteer intake, training and shift planning
  • A shared needs desk with local organisations

We don't

  • Distribute aid outside the coordination structure
  • Send untrained volunteers into the field
  • Fundraise using images of affected people
  • Search and rescue — that belongs to qualified teams

Area 03

Psychosocial support

Trauma responses often become visible weeks, sometimes months, after the event. So we do not confine support to the emergency phase: our programmes run with follow-up for at least 24 months.

Follow-up period
Minimum 24 months
Team
Psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers
Cost
All services are free

Get in touch for support

Support groups for children and adolescents

Play- and art-based group work in which a child is never pushed to talk and moves at their own pace. A parent briefing session is part of the programme.

Adult grief and adjustment work

Group and individual support through bereavement, displacement, and the move into permanent housing.

Secondary trauma support for responders

Post-shift decompression sessions and supervision for health workers, search and rescue teams, teachers and volunteers.

Referral network

Where clinical intervention is needed, an established referral protocol with the Barış Mental Health Hospital and university hospitals.

Area 04

Capacity building

There is a limit to what one organisation can do in the field. So we open our training material and response protocols to other organisations — with no condition other than attribution.

  • Training of trainers: a 40-hour programme for local NGO and municipal staff.
  • Shared protocols: field response and case management documents under an open licence.
  • Supervision: monthly group supervision for practitioners working in the field.
  • In-house training: disaster and trauma awareness for schools and workplaces.

Download the material from the resource centre

Area 05

Advocacy

We turn the problems we see recurring in the field into policy proposals. Our advocacy is not party politics; it offers data and field experience.

Our priority agenda

  • Defining post-disaster psychosocial support as a public service
  • Making child protection standards mandatory in temporary shelter areas
  • Increasing curriculum time given to practical disaster preparedness
  • Requiring registration, training and insurance for disaster volunteers
