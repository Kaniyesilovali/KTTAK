Emergency kit checklist

A kit makes the first 72 hours portable for the moment you have to leave home. You can build it over several weeks rather than in one go; an incomplete kit beats no kit at all.

Checklist 7 min read Print-ready CC BY-NC 4.0

The logic

Not one bag but two separate preparations

The go-bag (72 hours). It stands by the door, can be picked up with one hand and carried on your back. Be realistic about weight: a bag you cannot carry stays in the hallway. Eight to ten kilos for an adult, and two or three for a child carrying their own, are sensible limits.

Home supplies (one week). For when you can stay at home but the water and power are cut. Keep them on a kitchen shelf, separate from the go-bag so the two do not get mixed up in a hurry.

Put the bag not under a bed but as close to the door as possible, and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is. Leave a pair of sturdy shoes and a whistle beside it; in a night evacuation those are what people look for first.

Core list

What every household needs

Water and food

  • 3 litres of water per person per day — at least 3 litres in the bag, 7 days at home
  • Water purification tablets or drops
  • Food that keeps: nuts, dried fruit, biscuits, tinned food
  • A tin opener that needs no power
  • Energy bars or gels (small volume, high calorie)
  • Salt and sugar (for oral rehydration)

Light, power, communication

  • Torch and head lamp — do not use candles; gas leak risk
  • Spare batteries, bagged separately
  • A charged power bank and cables
  • Battery or wind-up radio
  • A whistle — shouting burns energy fast, a whistle does not
  • Spare charger and a car adapter

Health

  • First aid kit: sterile gauze, bandages, plasters, scissors, gloves
  • Regular medication — at least 7 days, in its packaging
  • Painkillers, fever reducer, anti-diarrhoeal
  • Antiseptic solution
  • Spare glasses, contact lens solution
  • Photographs of prescriptions and medical reports

Shelter and clothing

  • Foil emergency blanket — very light, very effective
  • Raincoat or tarpaulin
  • One change of clothes, thick socks
  • Sturdy closed shoes — beside the bag
  • Work gloves, dust mask
  • Thin blanket or sleeping bag

Hygiene

  • Wet wipes, hand sanitiser, soap
  • Toilet paper, rubbish bags
  • Sanitary pads / tampons
  • Toothbrush and toothpaste
  • Small towel

Documents and money

  • Copies of ID and passport in a waterproof bag
  • Copies of deeds, tenancy agreement, insurance policy
  • Health card, vaccination record
  • Small notes and coins — card readers may not work
  • A copy of your family disaster plan
  • Phone numbers of relatives — written on paper

A paper copy of your contacts looks unnecessary — until the phone dies. If you do not know a single number by heart, that sheet of paper is the most valuable thing in the bag.

By household

What to add

Standard lists are written for an average adult. The additions below are the items most often forgotten during an evacuation and most badly missed afterwards.

Babies and young children

A 72-hour baby bag

  • Formula, bottle, spare dummy; clean water set aside separately
  • Nappies, barrier cream, wet wipes (more than you think)
  • Spare clothes — more than one change
  • A sling or carrier — carrying in arms occupies both hands
  • A familiar toy or blanket — the soothing effect is real
  • A wristband with the child's name, date of birth and a carer's number

Older people and chronic conditions

Needs that cannot be interrupted

  • Seven days of medication with a written dose schedule
  • Blood pressure monitor, glucose meter, spare test strips
  • Spare glasses, hearing aid batteries, denture care kit
  • Walking stick or frame; non-slip shoes
  • A small cool bag for medication needing refrigeration
  • A written list of diagnoses, medication and the doctor's contact

Disabled household members

Equipment and communication

  • Wheelchair spares and a puncture repair kit
  • Chargers and spare batteries for devices; a plan for power cuts
  • A communication card or tablet if speech is difficult
  • Food, bowl and lead for a guide dog
  • Catheter or stoma care supplies
  • A personal support plan: who helps, and how transfers are done

Pets

For admission to shelters

  • A carrier — most shelter sites will not accept uncrated animals
  • Three days of food and water, a folding bowl
  • Lead, muzzle, spare harness
  • Copies of the vaccination record and microchip number
  • A photograph of you together (proof of ownership)
  • Cat litter or puppy pads, and bags

In the car

The small set that lives in the vehicle

  • 1.5 litres of water and food that keeps
  • Shoes, gloves, a foil blanket
  • Torch and power bank
  • A small first aid kit
  • A paper road map — for when the phone does not work
  • The habit of never letting the tank drop below half

Maintenance

Twenty minutes, twice a year

  • Refresh the water, check food dates
  • Check medication expiry and replace anything used
  • Take the batteries out and test them — a leaking cell ruins the whole bag
  • Fully charge the power bank
  • Update children's clothing and shoe sizes
  • Confirm document copies are still valid
  • Check the numbers on the family disaster plan

Use the clock changes as your reminder: twice a year, the same weekend. Put it in the calendar today.
